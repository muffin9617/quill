Evaluating AI SDRs Through Useful Customer Conversations

Assess AI outbound calling through list quality, latency, lead qualification and sales handoffs, with a look at the possibility of AI-to-AI conversations.

HSHan ShenB2B Digital & AI Marketing Architect 2026-10-29 About 5 min read

What makes an AI sales call useful?

During an AI SDR vendor presentation, a colleague asked when the technology would become so natural that a customer could no longer tell they were speaking to AI.

Voice, pauses and turn-taking certainly affect the experience. I wanted to ask a further question. Even if the customer cannot tell, what value does the call create?

Here I am discussing AI sales development representatives used for outbound phone calls, gathering initial needs, assessing whether a lead deserves further follow-up and passing relevant information to sales. This is one application within the wider field of sales automation.

My priority is whether the product can complete a useful conversation in an appropriate setting. A natural voice improves the experience, while list quality, purpose and follow-through determine what the call can accomplish.

Calling capacity and willingness to answer

An outbound tool can increase a company’s capacity to place calls. The customer still decides whether to answer and continue listening.

In my own experience, I already let an AI phone assistant answer some calls. Information can pass through that filter regardless of whether a person or a bot is calling. This is a personal observation with limited scope.

It suggests a tension worth considering. Companies may optimize for reaching more people, while customers optimize for avoiding unnecessary interruptions. A more natural voice may do little for a call that lacks relevance. Lower dialing costs therefore need to be evaluated alongside the cost of acquiring a qualified customer.

Examine the whole workflow

Contact volume and customer fit

The list is the starting point. AI can support research and the organization of public information. Finding a contact still leaves questions about suitability.

Does the company match the target profile? Does the contact hold a relevant responsibility? Is the information current? What value could the conversation offer them?

Evaluate research speed and list quality separately, with clear data sources and contact boundaries. Otherwise, automation can simply accelerate the processing of unsuitable contacts.

Test the conversation beyond a demonstration

Latency is central to my assessment. After the customer finishes speaking, how long does it take to provide a useful response? Can the system stop when interrupted? Does it mistake a brief pause for the end of a turn?

Google Cloud’s voice agent documentation discusses latency, end-of-speech detection and interruption settings. [1][2] These are aspects of the full conversation that deserve testing alongside response generation speed.

I would test ordinary questions, interruptions, corrections, ambiguous answers and out-of-scope requests. Observe whether the customer must repeat themselves, whether the response is relevant and whether uncertain issues reach a human.

Waiting time deserves attention, but its effect depends on the task, expectations and type of error. A universal conversion-loss figure for every extra second would require evidence beyond these observations.

Information that supports the next action

Call summaries, intent labels and follow-up tasks are promising areas to evaluate. Their output can be sampled and checked against the original conversation and existing process.

A fluent summary can still misclassify a lead. A willingness to receive information carries a different meaning from a clear purchase intention. Mentioning a product leaves budget and decision authority unresolved.

Compare the record with the recording and the customer’s words. Check needs, timing, objections and commitments. Sales acceptance and the amount of repeated questioning reveal whether the handoff is useful.

Define the value of assistance and autonomy

I am particularly interested in whether a system can independently collect needs and assess lead fit. That objective requires a different evaluation from helping a human SDR spend less time taking notes.

Human participation may still be part of good service design for complex explanations, customer commitments and exceptions.

For an assistant, ask how much repetitive work it removes and whether information quality improves. For an autonomous system, define the tasks it can finish, how errors are detected and when a handoff is necessary.

Include lead acceptance, follow-up outcomes, human rework and error-handling costs in the assessment. Call counts and duration alone provide an incomplete view.

When the receiving party is also AI

My experience with call screening raises a possible scenario in which a company’s outbound agent initially speaks with a customer’s answering agent. This is a scenario for exploration, with no established timeline or claim that it represents the industry’s eventual destination.

It introduces further design questions. Whom does the caller represent? What information may the answering agent disclose? Which response acknowledges receipt, and which grants permission for follow-up? When must the person participate?

Accurate information, clear authority and an agreed next action would become especially important in such exchanges.

Define a useful conversation before automating it

For a trial, I would choose a clearly bounded task and agree on completion, errors and handoff conditions. I would compare it with the existing process for similar tasks, checking whether rework offsets time saved and whether the receiving sales team accepts the lead quality.

The goal is to make a necessary conversation more accurate and effective, and to support an appropriate next action for the customer and the business. Willingness to participate and accurate understanding belong in the evaluation alongside technical performance.
